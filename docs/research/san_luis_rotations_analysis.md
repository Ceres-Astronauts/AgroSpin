### **1. Method "Productive Double Crop": Winter Wheat → Second-Crop Soybean (or Late Maize)**

* **What it is:** Instead of leaving the field fallow in winter or spending capital on an unharvested cover crop, **Wheat** is planted in autumn-winter (harvested for grain in late spring) and immediately followed in no-till by **second-crop Soybean** (or **Late Maize**) into wheat stubble for summer.
* It targets **two commercial grain harvests within one year** while keeping soil anchored during high August-September winds and leaving wheat residue to suppress summer soil evaporation.
  **Temperature patterns:**
  *Rating: Unfavorable*
  *Score: 2 (Low suitability)*
  *Justification: In the Villa Mercedes farming zone, agrometeorological frosts (<3 °C) span March to November on average. Waiting for wheat harvest delays planting of double-crop soybean or maize, exposing grain fill to early autumn frosts (recorded in Villa Mercedes as early as April). In central-western San Luis (San Luis capital) the frost-free window is longer (May to September), though strong continental thermal amplitude (15 °C) persists. (No local full-sequence trials).*
  **Heat stress:**
  *Rating: Unfavorable*
  *Score: 2 (Low suitability)*
  *Justification: Summers in San Luis average 30 to 33 °C maximum temperatures (rising toward the semiarid northwest) with frequent summer heat waves. Spring heat spikes stress wheat during grain fill. While delayed double-crop soybean or maize shifts reproductive stages past January peaks, under rainfed conditions combined heat and summer water deficit sharply penalize grain set and fill across both crops. (No local full-sequence trials).*
  **Precipitation:**
  *Rating: Poor*
  *Score: 1 (Unsuitable)*
  *Justification: San Luis's marked winter drought (75-80% of rain falls between October and March) severely restricts rainfed wheat, which depends almost entirely on autumn-stored soil water. Late-spring wheat harvest leaves a depleted soil profile just before planting double-crop soybean or late maize, compromising stand establishment and yield in a structurally water-limited province. Only viable under irrigation or in exceptionally wet years in the far east (650-700 mm).*
  **Soil moisture:**
  *Rating: Poor*
  *Score: 1 (Unsuitable)*
  *Justification: In San Luis sandy-loam soils, winter wheat consumes nearly all autumn-stored plant-available water, leaving a dry profile by November/December for second-crop soybean or late maize. Although wheat stubble reduces subsequent surface evaporation, insufficient pre-seeding recharge causes failure or heavy yield penalties in rainfed double crops. (Note: Where shallow water tables exist, such as Cuenca del Morro, wheat uptake contributes partially, yet annual cropping still lags behind perennial pastures).*
  **Evaporation:**
  *Rating: Moderately favorable*
  *Score: 3 (Moderately suitable)*
  *Justification: Year-round living crop cover and heavy wheat residue sharply curtail bare-soil evaporation during summer, channeling water into crop transpiration. Nevertheless, cumulative evapotranspiration demand of two harvested grain crops exceeds rainfed rainfall supply across San Luis, inducing high actual ET stress in the second crop except above shallow water tables.*
  **Frost risk:**
  *Rating: Poor*
  *Score: 1 (Unsuitable)*
  *Justification: Presents the highest cumulative frost exposure in central-eastern San Luis (Villa Mercedes):*
  *• Wheat: Late frosts between late October and November (within local standard deviation) during heading or flowering cause spikelet sterility and severe yield loss.*
  *• Second-crop soybean or maize: Planted late after wheat harvest, grain filling faces high risk of early autumn frosts (average first frost date around late March in Villa Mercedes), halting grain fill prior to physiological maturity.*

### **2. Method "Biological Pump Anti-Water-Table & Anti-Erosion": Multi-Year Alfalfa → Late Maize *(Cuenca del Morro / Sloped Fields Specialist)***

* **What it is:** Rotate 3 to 4 years of **Alfalfa** (deep-rooted perennial pasture maintaining year-round ground cover) followed by **Late Maize**, capturing accumulated soil organic matter and symbiotically fixed nitrogen.
* Tackles two critical San Luis constraints simultaneously: on sloped fields or areas with rising water tables (such as Cuenca del Morro), alfalfa acts as a biological pump consuming surplus water 365 days a year, halting water erosion while leaving biological fertility for subsequent maize.
  **Temperature patterns:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Established alfalfa tolerates winter cold and San Luis frosts (including Cuenca del Morro and Villa Mercedes) when matched with an appropriate dormancy group. When rotating into single-crop late maize (without waiting for a winter grain harvest), planting sits squarely inside the optimal summer window, avoiding both late spring seedling frosts and early autumn grain-fill frosts.*
  **Heat stress:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Perennial alfalfa develops deep root systems supporting sustained transpiration and stronger tolerance to summer heat spikes than shallow-rooted annuals, though mid-summer growth rate slows. Subsequent December-planted late maize schedules critical flowering in February, bypassing peak January temperatures and heat waves.*
  **Precipitation:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: In central-eastern San Luis and Cuenca del Morro (600-650 mm/yr), multi-year alfalfa captures spring-summer rainfall with high annual water consumption (600-800 mm), drawing down surplus water where rainfall exceeds annual-crop evapotranspiration. Transitioning to December-planted late maize aligns critical water demand (February) with summer rainfall, provided alfalfa is terminated early enough in spring to allow soil profile recharge.*
  **Soil moisture:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Excels where surplus moisture and rising water tables occur (Cuenca del Morro): deep alfalfa roots extract water across several meters of profile, lowering the water table and preventing waterlogging. Conversely, alfalfa leaves a dry profile after 3-4 years, requiring timely early-spring termination (spring fallow) to recharge soil water before late maize seeding.*
  **Evaporation:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Across the 3-4 year pasture phase, alfalfa maintains continuous canopy cover that minimizes bare-soil evaporation while maximizing year-round transpiration (high actual ET approaching atmospheric demand when tapping shallow water tables or spring-summer rain). In Cuenca del Morro, high consumptive transpiration is the primary mechanism halting groundwater rise. Residual pasture mulch moderates early evaporation in the following late maize.*
  **Frost risk:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Minimizes both frost extremes:*
  *• Multi-year alfalfa: Once established in autumn (avoiding frost on newly emerged seedlings), mature stands endure San Luis winter frosts without plant mortality, undergoing only temporary dormancy-driven growth pauses.*
  *• Late maize: Independent of prior winter grain harvest, allowing December seeding (after November late-frost risk) with adapted maturity hybrids reaching physiological maturity before April autumn frosts.*

### **3. Method 3 — Water-Resilience Rotation / Defensive Approach (Sorghum [Summer] → Vetch + Rye [Winter] → Sunflower [Summer])**

* **When activated:** When climate series and seasonal forecasts indicate dry years and elevated heat stress.
* **Why suggested:** Drastically reduces water demand; grain sorghum enters drought quiescence while deep-rooted sunflower extracts subsoil moisture from deeper horizons.
* **Benefits:** Hairy vetch fixes atmospheric nitrogen, rye builds organic matter and anchors topsoil against winter wind erosion, and the diversified sequence breaks continuous-soybean pest cycles.
  **Temperature patterns:**
  *Rating: Moderately favorable*
  *Score: 3 (Moderately suitable)*
  *Justification: The winter cover mix of vetch + rye adapts well to winter cold across semiarid San Luis when managed as a cover crop (terminated before grain). However, sorghum requires warm seedbeds for germination and is highly sensitive to late spring and early autumn frosts, while cold spring soils or plantings after mid-November depress sunflower yield.*
  **Heat stress:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Best adapted to warm extremes in San Luis: sorghum has higher physiological heat tolerance than maize; sunflower endures high summer thermal regimes (especially in early/mid planting dates); and autumn-winter vetch + rye generates surface mulch that buffers maximum soil surface temperatures for the subsequent summer crop. (No local full-sequence trials).*
  **Precipitation:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Best matched to San Luis rainfall seasonality and variability, particularly in semiarid belts (450-600 mm): both sorghum and sunflower demand less water and sustain greater yield stability than maize or soybean under erratic rains. The winter vetch + rye cover uses residual autumn moisture and is terminated early (September/October) so it does not consume early spring rains needed for sunflower. (No local full three-crop chain trials).*
  **Soil moisture:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Most water-conserving option for semiarid sandy soils without a shallow water table: vetch + rye cover shields topsoil against direct evaporation and boosts infiltration; when terminated on time in spring (September/October), it preserves plant-available water for sunflower. Moreover, sorghum and sunflower roots explore deeper moisture without exhausting the profile like a harvested winter cereal.*
  **Evaporation:**
  *Rating: Favorable*
  *Score: 4 (Highly suitable)*
  *Justification: Optimizes the transpiration-to-evaporation ratio in semiarid rainfed environments: persistent rye + vetch residue (aided by rye's higher C:N ratio) acts as a physical barrier against radiation and wind, sharply cutting direct soil evaporation during spring-summer. Both sorghum and sunflower also regulate stomatal conductance and water use efficiently under high evaporative demand.*
  **Frost risk:**
  *Rating: Moderately favorable*
  *Score: 3 (Moderately suitable)*
  *Justification: Balances low-risk phases with critical seeding-window adjustments:*
  *• Vetch + Rye: Cold-hardy over winter and terminated prior to flowering/grain, escaping late-frost economic loss.*
  *• Sorghum and Sunflower: Sorghum is vulnerable to late frosts if planted too early and early frosts if delayed; under heavy rye residue, sunflower planted too early in spring can experience heightened radiative frost risk on seedlings, though sunflower tolerates cool vegetative stages better than sorghum or maize.*

### **Core Evaluation Criteria & Scientific References**

* **Drought tolerance (Physiological resilience):** Crop ability to survive and hold a yield floor during prolonged summer or winter rainfall breaks, serving as a safety filter during La Nina anomalies or heat waves (based on FAO yield-response Ky factors and INTA San Luis rainfed stability trials).
* **Water demand (Total cycle ETc vs. field supply):** Cumulative seasonal ET (mm) compared against expected rainfall plus stored soil profile water on low-retention sandy-loam soils (`ETc = ET0 * Kc`, FAO-56).
* **Soil adaptation (Physical and chemical compatibility):** Alignment with topsoil texture (sandy-loam to silty-loam), effective depth, absence of compacted layers, pH, and salinity/EC (FAO ECOCROP and INTA soil surveys).
* **Rotation contribution (Medium-term systemic value):** Surface residue persistence, biological N fixation (BNF), soil organic carbon (SOC) additions, and disruption of weed/pest/disease cycles (USDA NRCS Conservation Crop Rotation Standard 328).
* **Drainage (Waterlogging and surface crusting tolerance):** Sensitivity to root hypoxia under temporary ponding after torrential summer storms or rising water tables such as Cuenca del Morro (exclusion screen for sensitive species such as alfalfa or peanut on poorly drained sites).
